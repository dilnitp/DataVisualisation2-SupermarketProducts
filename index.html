Price, Shelf, Plate

What packaged food in Australia looks like, from the shop to the plate.

This page covers packaged food and drink only.

1

The shop

First, what are we paying?

Food prices rose everywhere

Prices rose in every capital city.

Hobart rose most, at 3.6%.

Canberra rose least, at 2.4%.

This shows how fast prices rose. It does not show which city costs more.

2

The shelf

Next, what fills the aisles?

Sweet snacks fill the biggest aisle

Tile size shows how many products.

Darker tiles score poorly more often.

Nearly 9 in 10 sweet snacks score D or E.

Nutri-Score runs from A (best) to E (worst). Colour counts only products that have a score.

Most scored products are ultra-processed

63 of every 100 are ultra-processed.

These are made mostly from industrial ingredients.

Only 8,386 products have a processing score.

Many products sell overseas too

About 6,000 are also listed in other countries.

New Zealand shares the most, then France.

Based on all 80,892 Australian products in the database. Contributors choose where a product is sold, so treat this as a guide. Lines show links, not shipping routes.

3

The label

Then, what is inside?

Where sugar, fat and salt hide

7 in 10 sweet snacks are high in sugar.

Sauces and salty snacks carry the salt.

‘High’ follows UK traffic-light labels. Drinks are left out.

Most products are low in sugar and salt

About 6 in 10 sit in the bottom-left corner.

That corner means under 10 g sugar and 1.5 g salt per 100 g. A few products above 60 g sugar or 4 g salt are not shown.

Sweet snacks vary widely in sugar

Most other groups bunch near zero.

Sweet snacks stretch far to the right.

Ridge height is scaled within each group. Products above 60 g sugar are not shown.

Each group has a nutrient fingerprint

Lines join typical amounts per 100 g.

Each axis runs from lowest to highest group.

Hover over a line to follow it.

Who tops each nutrient?

Sweet snacks rank first for sugar and saturated fat.

Sauces and salty snacks tie for salt.

Typical means the middle value. Where groups tie, they are listed A to Z.

4

The plate

Finally, what do we eat?

Most adults miss the fruit target

Tasmania has the highest share, at 60%.

Victoria has the lowest, at 55%.

Adults, 2022, adjusted for age. The daily target depends on age and sex.

Vegetables are missed even more

About 9 in 10 adults fall short.

Queensland is worst. Tasmania is best.

Adults, 2022, adjusted for age. Each state is drawn the same size.

In short

Food prices keep rising.

Many packaged products score poorly.

Most of us still miss fruit and vegetables.

About this page

Data and sources

  • Products: Open Food Facts, Australian products, downloaded October 2026. A crowd-sourced database, so it does not list every product. Licence: Open Database Licence (ODbL).
  • World map: world-atlas (Natural Earth, public domain), simplified at 1:110 million.
  • State maps: Australian Bureau of Statistics, ASGS Edition 3 digital boundary files, States and Territories 2021. Simplified for web use.
  • Diet: Australian Bureau of Statistics, National Health Survey 2022, Table 2.3 (proportions, adjusted for age).
  • Prices: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Consumer Price Index, Australia, food and non-alcoholic beverages by capital city. Change is August 2026 compared with August 2025.
  • ‘High’ levels: UK traffic-light thresholds per 100 g, from Food Standards Scotland guidance: sugar over 22.5 g, saturated fat over 5 g, salt over 1.5 g. These are not Australian rules.

How to read the scores

  • Nutri-Score grades foods from A (best) to E (worst). Open Food Facts calculates it from the nutrition label.
  • Processing level uses the NOVA groups: 1 minimally processed, 2 processed ingredients, 3 processed, 4 ultra-processed.
  • Typical means the middle value of the group.

Choices and limits

  • 14,100 products in nine food and drink groups are shown. Alcohol is left out.
  • Values that are impossible for 100 g (for example, sugar above 100 g) were removed.
  • The price map shows how fast prices rose, not price levels.
  • Survey results are estimates.
